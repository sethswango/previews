A Rising Change: getting found on Google

An SEO writeup from Seth Swango for Kidsister · August 2026 · arisingchange.com

The short version

The writing on this site is genuinely good, the specialty is real, and the prices are honest. Most of what is holding it back is configuration: a few structural signals Google reads that are currently switched off. Those are cheap fixes, and they come first. The bigger opportunity is positioning. Savannah is licensed across all of Missouri and works entirely by video, but the site mostly reads as a St. Louis practice. And her strongest specialty, queer-affirming perinatal care, is stated nowhere as a single clear claim, even though almost nobody else in the state is making it.

The five things that matter most:

  • Seven of the eight main pages have no main heading (H1). That heading is the strongest signal telling Google what a page is about. It is a Squarespace text setting, not a writing problem.
  • The homepage title tag is just “a rising change.” Fifteen characters, no keywords. The strongest page on the site is an easy win: give it a title that says what she does and where.
  • The practice serves all of Missouri but the site reads as St. Louis. Every one of Missouri's 99 rural counties is a federally designated mental health shortage area. A statewide telehealth practice is one of the few answers to that, and saying so is a straightforward opportunity.
  • The perinatal page, her most differentiated service, is the least optimized. It has no meta description and no main heading. It is also the page with the least competition.
  • The clearest open position: queer-affirming perinatal care in Missouri. We checked the closest competitor's perinatal page: it speaks only to “moms” and “women.” Savannah has the training (Postpartum Support International), the affirming practice, and the statewide license. The site just never says all three in one place.

Where we suggest the 10 copywriting hours go: rewrite the homepage around a real search headline, deepen the three service pages, and add two new pages (an out-of-network explainer and a statewide telehealth page). A page-by-page brief for the copywriting is included separately.

What we checked and how

We crawled the raw page source of all 10 pages on the site, the sitemap, and robots.txt, then measured six competing Missouri practices the same way and ran the target searches live to see who actually appears. Where this writeup states a fact about a site, it was measured. Where it estimates impact, it says so.

One honest limit: we did not have Google Search Console or Analytics access for this pass, so we can say what the site is and how it compares, but not which searches it already ranks for or what traffic converts. That data exists (or can, in about 10 minutes of setup) and it is the single best upgrade to the next round of this work. Details at the end.

What is already working (so we leave it alone)

The site is fast: server response beat the best technical competitor we measured, and images are served responsively. Sitemap and canonical setup are correct. Two service pages (therapy for women, therapy for queer or questioning women) already have well-written search titles. Google Analytics is installed. The brand search “a rising change counseling” returns the site at number one. And the voice of the copy is a real asset: lines like “overwhelmed, overworked, overlooked. over it.” are distinctive in a field of interchangeable therapy sites. Speed and hosting need no money spent on them.

The technical fixes, in order

1. Give every page a main heading (H1)

Where it stands. Only the services and fees page has one. The visible headings on the other seven pages are set as smaller heading levels, so the top-level signal is simply absent. Set the primary heading block on each page to Heading 1 in the Squarespace editor. No design change needed.

Also. On the queer and questioning page, a line break splits one heading into two separate blocks (“therapy for lesbian, bisexual,” and “queer or questioning women”). Rejoin it as a single Heading 1.

2. Retitle the pages that have no target

Where it stands. The homepage title is the brand name alone. Suggested: “Online Therapy for Women and Queer Adults in Missouri | A Rising Change.” The perinatal page should carry its location and specialty: “Perinatal and Postpartum Therapy in Missouri | PMAD Specialist | A Rising Change.” The about page should carry the credential: “Savannah Helton, LCSW | Queer-Affirming Therapist in Missouri.” The two service pages that were already done well should be left alone.

3. Write the missing meta description on the perinatal page

Where it stands. That page has none, so Google composes its own snippet, and on a symptoms-heavy page the auto-snippet can easily be a list of depression symptoms. A drafted description is in the copy brief.

4. Add structured data

Where it stands. The only markup present is the Squarespace default. Three additions: business markup on the homepage naming the service area as the state of Missouri (this is how a telehealth practice tells Google it is not just one address), person markup for Savannah with her license and credentials, and FAQ markup on the FAQ page, which already has 900+ words of genuine questions and answers and can become eligible for expanded search listings. Best effort-to-reward item on the list.

5. Small conversion fixes, minutes each

Where it stands. The phone number and email appear as plain text with no tap-to-call or mail links, and most therapy searches happen on phones. The favicon is still the Squarespace default. Every button routes through the contact page; the free consultation buttons could link straight to her booking portal. And Google Analytics has no conversion events set up, so nobody can yet say which pages produce consultations. Each of these is a small change.

6. Consistency details worth a quick confirm

Where it stands. Google holds health sites to a higher documentation standard than other content, so small consistency items carry unusual weight here. Years of experience reads differently across the site and directories (13, 15+, and 20 in different places); one true number everywhere is the fix. The Psychology Today profile shows a different phone number than the site, which may be an intentional tracking number and is just worth confirming. The “1 in 5” PMAD statistic on the perinatal page is good content and would benefit from a source citation.

The open position

Queer-affirming perinatal care in Missouri, delivered statewide by telehealth. We verified rather than assumed: the strongest Missouri perinatal competitor writes only to “mothers” and “women” with no inclusive framing anywhere, and the one small practice that does specialize nearby (Missouri and Kansas, queer family building) has a five-page site with no markup. Postpartum Support International, the body Savannah trained with, runs dedicated queer and trans parent programs nationally, so the demand is documented. Savannah can already claim every part of this sentence: a licensed Missouri clinician, trained in perinatal mood and anxiety disorders through PSI, explicitly queer-affirming, available anywhere in the state by video. The recommendation is simply to say it, prominently, on the perinatal page and the homepage.

Worth naming: how prominently to state this is Savannah's call to make, and the copy should follow her comfort level.

Where the 10 copywriting hours go

A separate page-by-page brief covers exact titles, headings, and word targets. The shape of it:

  • Homepage rewrite (about 2 hours). New heading naming what she does and where; keep “care. connection. change.” as the tagline beneath it. Grow to 700 to 900 words: who she serves, fully virtual statewide, the out-of-network model stated plainly, free consultation as the one next step.
  • Perinatal page (about 1.5 hours, highest strategic value). Add the missing description and heading, grow toward 1,000 to 1,200 words, explicitly welcome queer parents, non-birthing parents, and partners alongside moms, and give her PSI training and seven years with high-risk pregnancies the prominence they earn.
  • The two existing service pages (about 2 hours). Headings plus depth: what sessions look like, common concerns, an explicit statewide telehealth line. Titles on both are already good and stay.
  • New page: out-of-network and fees, plainly explained (about 1.5 hours). “Do you take insurance” is one of the most-searched therapy questions and she has a genuinely good answer: superbills, tiered equity fees, FSA/HSA. Includes a step-by-step script for calling your insurer. Context worth knowing: the St. Louis average is around $200 a session and she charges $125 to $135; that is an accessibility story, not a discount story.
  • New page: therapy anywhere in Missouri (about 1.5 hours). One honest statewide page naming the regions she serves and what a video session is like. One page, written for real, not a batch of near-identical city pages, which is a known spam pattern and would put rankings at risk.
  • Quick passes (about 1 hour). FAQ heading and structure so the markup can go in, about-page title and credential text.

Analytics: what exists and what to set up

Already in place: Google Analytics (we found the tag installed), Squarespace's built-in stats, her booking system (Sessions Health) which quietly holds the true conversion record, and Psychology Today's profile stats. Missing: Google Search Console, which is free, takes about 10 minutes, and records the exact searches the site appears for. We can set it up given either a Squarespace contributor invite or access to the Analytics property.

Also worth doing: turn on conversion events in Analytics for the contact form and booking clicks, and add one intake question in Sessions Health (“how did you hear about me?”), which builds a marketing record no tool can match.

What success looks like for this practice, concretely: a solo caseload runs around 20 to 25 weekly clients, so a full practice needs roughly 2 to 4 well-matched new clients a month. At $125 a session over a typical course of therapy, one client relationship is worth roughly $1,900 to $3,100. The measurements that matter are consultations requested and consultations that become clients, not raw traffic. With Search Console live for 90 days, the next pass of this work moves from good judgment to exact numbers.

One decision that belongs to Savannah

The site currently blocks AI assistants (ChatGPT, Claude, and others) from reading it. That is the Squarespace default and the norm among her competitors too; five of the seven sites we checked do the same. It only matters because a growing share of people now ask an AI assistant for therapist recommendations, and a blocked site cannot be recommended. “Queer-affirming perinatal therapist in Missouri” is exactly the kind of specific, sensitive question people bring to an assistant instead of a search box, so allowing it would currently be a differentiator. Some clinicians prefer to stay blocked on principle, and that is a legitimate choice. Flagging it so the decision is made on purpose rather than by default.
